# 2. Introduction:
A Common Source (CS) amplifier with a resistor load is a basic and widely used configuration in analog electronics for voltage amplification. In this circuit, an NMOS transistor acts as the amplifying device while a resistor connected at the drain functions as the load. When an input signal is applied to the gate, variations in gate voltage cause changes in the drain current, which produces an amplified voltage at the output. This type of amplifier is simple to design and helps in understanding fundamental concepts such as voltage gain, biasing, and frequency response, making it useful for studying analog circuit behavior.

# 4. Theory:
The circuit consists of:

• NMOS transistor acting as the amplifying device

• PMOS transistor acting as the active load

• Source degeneration resistor Rs

• Bias voltage VB

• Output node at the drain

**PMOS Active Load:**

Instead of using a passive resistor as the load, a **PMOS transistor** is used as an active load. The PMOS transistor provides a higher output resistance compared to a normal resistor, which helps increase the voltage gain of the amplifier. This makes the amplifier more efficient and suitable for integrated circuit designs.

**Source Degeneration:**

A **source resistor (Rs)** is connected to the source of the NMOS transistor to introduce **negative feedback**. This feedback helps control the current flowing through the transistor and improves the stability of the circuit.

**Effects:**

* Improves the linearity of the amplifier
* Stabilizes the operating (bias) point
* Slightly reduces the overall voltage gain

Thus, the **PMOS active load** helps in achieving higher gain, while **source degeneration** improves stability and linear operation, though it slightly lowers the gain.

The **simulated gain (21.86  dB)** is smaller than the **theoretical gain (23.74 dB)** because theoretical calculations usually assume ideal conditions with very large output resistance. In practical simulation, effects like **channel length modulation** reduce the output resistance of the transistors. Additionally, **parasitic capacitances** and accurate device model parameters lower the effective gain. The presence of the **source degeneration resistor (Rs)** also decreases the overall voltage gain of the amplifier.

# 12. Inference:
The difference between theoretical and simulated results occurs due to practical MOSFET effects such as channel length modulation, mobility degradation, and parasitic capacitances. These non-ideal factors also introduce a dominant pole that limits the amplifier’s high-frequency response. Although source degeneration reduces gain slightly, it improves stability and linearity, and the amplifier still meets the expected performance.
